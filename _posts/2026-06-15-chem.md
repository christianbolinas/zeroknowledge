# Don't Fear The Derivation
First off, metrics: I increased rigor (arguably; the previous teacher was pretty focused on well-motivated "chemistry is
all around us," but I had them doing heavy unit analysis and mathematical derivations), while increasing scores by an
average (per student) of __14 points__. I also eliminated homework (because I'm not interested in reading ChatGPT
output). I attribute this increase in rigor partially to me relentlessly cold-calling students (indirectly: it gives me
a good picture on what to speed through versus what to spend extra time reviewing tomorrow, but mainly because it keeps
them focused and not talking or taking out their phones, because it is not pleasant if you miss a cold call in my
classroom), but also to the way that I teach derivations (which, being a math-adjacent science guy, is the height of
rigor, in my opinion).

I majored in chemistry and then took a lot of graduate classes in computer science: I've seen and been tested on plenty
of derivations. I don't like how they're taught. In my experience, they're taught in a very formal, academic way: "if
you start with these seemingly meaningless assumptions, and then do a whiteboard worth of complicated algebraic
manipulation, you can stop here. This equally unmotivated equation is named after some dude and describes how something
behaves."

What I had a lot of success with (the last unit I taught was on the kinetic theory of gases, and it had the highest test
averages) was teaching derivations in a very intuitive way, by just treating them as a formalization of what we know
about real-world objects. I'll use the derivation of the Van Der Waals equation from nothing (real-world observations
and middle school science class) as an example: I did it over the course of five class periods, teaching 20ish of 47
minutes each, but if 16 year olds can get it in less than two hours, readers of a theoretical machine learning blog can
get it in one post.

## Sports Betting, Models, and Assumptions
I introduced the concept quantitative modeling with sports (betting). My school just won a state championship in
women's basketball (with six of 12 team members being in my honors section!) and is very athletics-heavy, so it landed
_slightly better_ than if I just jumped into the equations.

> My students seem to like "edgy" topics. They seemed marginally more intrinsically interested when I motivated
acid-base neutralizations by discussing why many rap songs mention baking soda. 

> They were mainly interested in asking if I know how to make XYZ drug, though.

We might want to model the winner of a basketball game with some equation based on statistics we have at halftime (to
make bets as to the winner). I started cold-calling here: one statistic might be 3pt%: we can model the winner as being
the team that was more accurate from deep at the half. Another statistic might be turnover differential: we can model
the winner as turning the ball over less. Of course, an even better model than those two could be one that uses the
information than both.

> This is not enough to make you into an expert quant sports gambler. Obviously.

## Gases
We just modeled the winner of a basketball game based on data we've collected at the half. My class is about chemistry,
not sports analytics, though, so we're going to talk about modeling gases.

> If you feel like being a wiseass, you can say that this is technically physics. You're wrong. It's physical chemistry,
which is a discipline of chemistry.

Some data we could collect regarding the "circumstances" around a gas are (I kept cold-calling to brainstorm here):

- its pressure,
- the size of the container it's in, which is called "volume,"
- the number of gas molecules in the container, and
- the temperature inside the container of gas.

I then started talking about modeling gas behavior using tires. Like, on a car. 

I first asked them if airing up a tire to increase its pressure changed the amount of oxygen molecules (yes, I know that
air is mostly nitrogen) inside, and as a follow-up, if it increased or decreased that amount. (I put both times to a
class vote; obviously, I led the witness in both cases, and they unanimously voted "increase.") To that, I wrote
"gas pressure is proportional to the number of molecules," then wrote $p \propto n$.

Next, I asked them if tires have higher pressure in the summer or winter, and they unanimously voted "summer." To that,
again, I wrote "gas pressure is proportional to temperature," then wrote $p \propto T$.

Lastly, I asked them if squeezing a tire, making it smaller, increases or decreases its pressure; to that, they
unanimously voted "increases"; I wrote "as volume decreases, pressure increases," then wrote $p \propto \frac{1}{V}$.

So these are more like qualitative models: you can say that "a hot gas is higher pressure than a cold gas, with volume
and the number of molecules the same for both."

## What is proportionality? The Ideal Gas Law
I then got into how "proportional to," which is that $\propto$ symbol, means "equal to some number times it." Like, if I
say that someone's weight is proportional to their height, it's $w \propto h$. We can change that "proportional to"
symbol to an "equals" symbol by putting some number to multiply one side of the equation by. I then explained that I'm
68" tall and about 185 pounds, then wrote:

$$w \propto h$$

$$w = 2.72 h$$

I then mentioned that you can combine those proportionalities: if pressure is proportional to both temperature and the
amount of molecules, it's proportional to their product, just like how we combined models to make a more accurate model
for our basketball sports betting. From there, I derived the ideal gas law:

$$p \propto n \land p \propto T \implies p \propto nT$$

$$p \propto nT \land p \propto \frac{1}{V} \implies p \propto \frac{nT}{V}$$

$$\implies pV \propto nT$$

$$\implies pV = nRT$$

> I explained that $R$ is a quantity found with experiments, just like how my weight in pounds is 2.72 times my height
in inches.

## Assumptions of the Ideal Gas Law
I then explained how the ideal gas law is a model, just like our sports betting model, and it makes assumptions, like
all models. I explained that two assumptions the ideal gas law makes are that

1. gas molecules have no volume/size, and that
2. gas molecules are not attracted to each other.

> I figured another "brainstorm-and-vote" would've been asking a bit too much from my class.

I had an aside about how violating them would make it less accurate, then cold called them on if it's more accurate for
Xenon or Radon, and for examples of forces that would attract molecules together.

## Correction 1: Volume
The way I explained this by basically asserting that a gas molecule took up space, just a really small amount. 

> None of them found this objectionable.

I then said that we should add this to Boyle's law to get another, better model for volume and pressure: the volume a
gas takes up is proportional to the inverse of its pressure, as well as the volume of the molecules itself.

$$ V_{text{better}} = V - \text{volume of the molecules} $$

$$ V \propto \frac{1}{P} $$

$$V_{\text{better}} \propto \frac{1}{p} + \text{volume of the molecules}$$

I then said that the volume of the molecules is equal to the __b__igness (size) of one molecule, times the number of
molecules themselves:

$$
\text{volume of the molecules} = nb $$

$$V_{\text{better}} \propto \frac{1}{p} + nb
$$

Then, we could substitute that $V{text{better}}$ into the ideal gas law, to get:

$$
PV_{\text{better}} = nRT
$$

## What is pressure, anyway? Correction 2: Pressure
Then, I got a bit into what pressure is. I said that gas particles rapidly float around and sometimes crash into things.
Pressure is basically the total rate by which the rapidly-moving gas particles crash into the container they're in; this
is dampened by intermolecular attraction (whether hydrogen bonding or London dispersion), because the crowd of molecules
pulls one that's bound to crash into the wall back towards them, a little:

$$
P_{\text{better}} = P - \text{pressure lost to dampening}
$$

This pressure that gets lost to dampening is roughly the product of the dampening per molecule and the number of
collisions that are available to actually get dampened:

$$
\text{pressure lost to dampening} \propto \text{dampening per molecule} * \text{amount of collisions (that got
dampened)}
$$

I explained that the intermolecular attraction, which is responsible for the dampening per molecule is proportional to
the density of the (other) molecules in the container (which is itself the number of molecules per volume $\frac{n}{V}$: 

$$
\text{dampening per molecule} \propto \frac{n}{V}
$$

And the amount of collisions (which matters: if there's more collisions, there's more dampening, which means more
pressure lost to dampening) is _also_ proportional to the density of the molecules in the container:

$$
\text{amount of collisions (that got dampened)} \propto \frac{n}{V}
$$

So we can say:

$$
\text{pressure lost to dampening} \propto \frac{n}{V} * \frac{n}{V} = \frac{an^2}{V^2}
$$

And finally, 
$$
P_{\text{better}} = P - \frac{an^2}{V^2}
$$

## Derivation, again
We can just plug those two expressions of $P_{\text{better}}$ and $V_{\text{better}}$ into the ideal gas law. That gives
us an overall more accurate model:

$$
(P - \frac{an^2}{V^2})(V - nb) = nRT
$$

There's your Van Der Waals equation. Not super difficult to find!
